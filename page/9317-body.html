Emission Factor Licences: What You Can Actually Republish

[gc_pin_card alt="Emission factor licences — what you can actually republish, across 137 sources" desc="We read the licence terms of all 137 sources in our corpus. 42% use no standard licence at all. Which sources permit republication, which do not, the publishers’ own reasons, and the five traps that catch working teams."]

Every carbon product is built on somebody else’s numbers, and almost every team assumes those numbers are either “open” or “not open”. We read the terms of all 137 sources in our corpus, one at a time, and that binary does not survive contact with the evidence. Forty-two of them use no recognisable licence at all — just bespoke wording you have to read and interpret. This page is what we found, including the sources we hold and may not republish, and the reasons in the publishers’ own words.

Quick Answer

Most major emission-factor sources — DEFRA, EPA, IPCC, Ember, ADEME — permit republication with attribution. Standards bodies (ISO, EN, CDP, IFRS) do not. The trap is in between: several sources let you use a number but not redistribute the dataset, and those are different permissions.

Licence review is the least glamorous job in building a carbon product and the one most likely to be discovered late, by a lawyer, after the feature has shipped. It is not hard work. It is just work that has to be done once per source, re-done on every new edition, and recorded somewhere a future colleague can find.

We have done it 137 times for our own corpus. The results below are generated from that record rather than written by hand, so the tables cannot quietly drift away from what the API actually serves.

Key Point

You cannot triage emission-factor sources by licence badge, because 42% of them do not have one. Every source needs its terms read once, and the answer recorded next to the data rather than in somebody’s inbox.

By row count the picture is friendlier than by source count: 15,748 of 17,074 rows — 92% — come from sources that permit republication. The restrictive sources are mostly small: a standards table here, a research paper there. That asymmetry is worth knowing, because it means the licence problem is rarely about volume. It is about the specific rows you happen to need.

Four permissions people treat as one

“Can I use this?” is four separate questions, and a source can answer them differently.

The four permissions, and what each one actually governs
PermissionThe question it answers
UseMay I read this and compute with it, internally?
Publish a resultMay a number derived from it appear in a report I publish?
RedistributeMay I republish the dataset itself, in bulk, to my users?
CommercialMay I do any of the above inside a product I charge for?

Two real examples from our own corpus show why the distinction matters.

ecoinvent answers differently within a single source: the methodology documentation is open, the quantitative data is not. You may describe how it works and cite it; you may not ship the numbers.

The GHG Protocol Product Standard is published with attribution on terms close to CC BY 4.0 — and is still not redistributable by a paid product, because the non-commercial condition bites on the fourth question rather than the third. A team that checks only “is it CC BY?” gets this one wrong.

Five traps that catch working teams

1. Share-alike is viral over your output

CC BY-SA does not merely require attribution — it can oblige you to license derived work on the same terms. For an emission-factor dataset that potentially reaches the results your product computes, not just the data you shipped. Four sources in our corpus carry share-alike terms, and it is the reason none of them are in the open export.

2. NonCommercial means your product, not your intent

A CC BY-NC source is unusable inside anything you charge for, however worthy the use. SBTi, PCAF, WBCSD Pathfinder and the GHG Protocol Product Standard all carry a non-commercial condition. PCAF adds NoDerivatives, so you may not even transform it.

3. “Free to download” is not “free to redistribute”

CDP, IFRS S2 and several industry bodies publish openly and charge nothing to read. That is a distribution decision, not a licence grant. The copyright is unchanged and the terms usually say so somewhere less prominent than the download button.

4. Paid standards are cited, never copied

ISO 14040, 14044, 14067 and EN 15978 sit behind BSI, DIN and AFNOR paywalls. You may implement the method and reference the standard. Reproducing its tables is a licence breach, and it is the one most likely to be noticed, because standards bodies watch for it.

5. Silence is not permission

The sharpest case in our corpus is ICAO’s Carbon Emissions Calculator, at 429 rows the largest single exclusion. Version 13.1 carries no copyright notice, no licence and no terms of use. Nothing forbids redistribution — and nothing grants it. An absent licence is the most restrictive outcome, not the most permissive, because the default under copyright is reserved.

The source we held back although we were allowed

One entry in our corpus is flagged CC BY 4.0 by the publisher’s own metadata, and we exclude it anyway.

An IEA dataset on AI and data-centre energy arrives labelled with an open licence. Our earlier licence audit of the IEA recorded a general prohibition on third-party publication of derived footprints. The label and the audit disagree, and we have not resolved it with the publisher yet.

Why this is in the generator, not a to-do list

The exclusion is implemented as an override that can only ever remove a source, never add one. A mistake in it costs us coverage; it cannot cause a breach. That asymmetry is the whole design: when a licence question is genuinely unresolved, the cheap failure has to be the one you build toward.

How we read the terms, and how this stays current

Each source was reviewed once when it was ingested, and is re-reviewed on each new edition, because terms change — a source cleared in 2025 can carry a non-commercial condition in 2026. Four things get recorded: the licence name, the publisher, whether republication is permitted, and the sentence that decides it.

That record travels with the data. Every API response carries the licence of every source it touched, so a downstream consumer never has to come back to this page to find out what they may do with a value they already hold.

The tables above are generated from that record rather than maintained by hand, which is the only way a page like this stays true. A hand-written licence table is accurate on the day it is published and quietly wrong within a year.

If you are doing this yourself

Record the deciding sentence, not your conclusion. “Redistributable: no” is useless to the colleague who inherits it in eighteen months. “NonCommercial clause, section 4b” can be re-checked in a minute when the publisher issues a new edition.
